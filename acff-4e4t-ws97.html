Reading Passage

Elementary · ~420 words · 2 min read

Stretching for thousands of kilometres across northern China, the Great Wall is one of the most famous structures ever built by human hands. Although many people imagine it as a single, continuous wall, the Great Wall is actually a vast network of walls, towers, and fortifications built over many centuries by different Chinese dynasties.

Construction of early wall sections began more than 2,000 years ago, during the Qin Dynasty, under Emperor Qin Shi Huang. The emperor ordered that existing smaller walls be connected into a single defensive barrier to protect China's northern border from invasions by nomadic tribes. This early work involved hundreds of thousands of labourers, including soldiers, prisoners, and ordinary peasants.

The most famous and well-preserved sections that tourists visit today were built by the later Ming Dynasty, between the fourteenth and seventeenth centuries. The Ming rulers rebuilt and strengthened the wall using brick and stone, creating the impressive structure with watchtowers, ramps, and battlements that most people picture when they think of the Great Wall.

The wall served several important purposes. Its primary function was military defence, allowing soldiers to monitor enemy movement and respond quickly using a system of signal fires and smoke beacons. Beyond defence, the wall also helped control trade and immigration along China's borders.

Building the wall came at a tremendous human cost. Historians estimate that hundreds of thousands, possibly millions, of workers died during its construction due to harsh weather, difficult terrain, and dangerous working conditions. Because of this, the wall is sometimes referred to as the longest cemetery on Earth.

Today, the Great Wall attracts millions of visitors every year and was officially named a UNESCO World Heritage Site in 1987. Ongoing preservation projects aim to protect the wall for future generations.
